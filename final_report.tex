\noindent{\large\bfseries Abstract}\par
\smallskip
\noindent We study whether YouTube trailer comments posted before a movie's release predict its eventual Letterboxd audience rating. We frame the problem as a 5-class classification task and build an end-to-end pipeline (TMDB $\to$ YouTube $\to$ Letterboxd) producing a dataset of 173 movies (2020--2024) with $\approx$236{,}000 pre-release comments. We compare six model variants: a majority baseline, TF-IDF + logistic regression, structured features (logistic regression and random forest), frozen DistilBERT embeddings + LR, and two end-to-end fine-tuned DistilBERT variants (per-comment and movie-level). Our best model is TF-IDF + LR at \textbf{0.423 accuracy / 0.401 macro F1} on 5-class and \textbf{0.538 / 0.522} on the collapsed 3-class task. Neither fine-tuning approach beats TF-IDF: pseudo-label noise hurts the per-comment variant, and the 121-movie training set is too small for stable end-to-end fine-tuning. A per-window temporal ablation confirms the proposal's hypothesis that \emph{late}-window comments carry the most signal, and a Pearson correlation of $r{=}0.27$ links our hype--negativity score to the actual rating bucket.

\section{Introduction}
\noindent YouTube trailer comments capture genuine pre-release reactions---tone, cast, premise---but they are noisy: hype, memes, sarcasm, and bots all coexist. This project asks whether enough signal survives the noise to predict how a film is eventually received.

\paragraph{Task.} For movie $m$ with pre-release trailer comments $C_m = \{c_1,\dots,c_n\}$ and post-release Letterboxd rating $s_m \in [0.5, 5.0]$, we map $s_m$ to a bucket $y_m \in \{0,1,2,3,4\}$ (\textit{bad} to \textit{great}) and learn $f(C_m) \to y_m$. We also report a coarser 3-class collapse $\{0,1\}\to\textit{neg},\,\{2\}\to\textit{neu},\,\{3,4\}\to\textit{pos}$.

\paragraph{Contributions.} (i) A reproducible 173-movie dataset with strict pre-release filtering. (ii) Six-model comparison covering lexical, hand-engineered, frozen-transformer, and two fine-tuned-transformer regimes. (iii) Adoption of mean absolute label distance (MAE) alongside accuracy and macro F1 to capture the ordinal structure of the buckets. (iv) Per-window temporal ablation showing late-stage comments dominate. (v) An expectation--reality analysis correlating pre-release hype with post-release ratings, with named outliers.

\paragraph{Updates since the midterm.} Per reviewer feedback we (a) implemented end-to-end fine-tuning with a [CLS] head (no pooling) for two distinct setups, (b) added MAE as an ordinal-aware metric for every model, (c) ran a per-window temporal ablation, and (d) added the expectation--reality and error-analysis sections.

\section{Related Work}
\noindent Sentiment-driven prediction of film outcomes has a long lineage. \cite{pang2002} showed bag-of-words classifiers compete with more complex methods, especially in low-data regimes---a finding our results echo. \cite{asur2010} used Twitter to forecast opening-weekend revenue, but Letterboxd ratings track \emph{long-run audience quality} rather than first-weekend revenue.

For text classification, \cite{devlin2019} and the distilled variant \cite{sanh2019} have become standard, but their advantage often vanishes when labelled data is scarce \cite{pang2002}, which is the regime we operate in.

\section{Data Collection}
\noindent The pipeline is modular: each stage is a standalone, resumable script.

\paragraph{Movie selection.} We queried the TMDB Discover API for English-language films released 2020--2024 with popularity $\geq 5$ and vote count $\geq 200$, retaining titles with at least 2 official YouTube trailers. This yielded 388 candidates. We stratified by year and popularity to sample 150 initially, later expanding to 210 for stronger statistical power.

\paragraph{YouTube comments.} Using YouTube Data API v3, we scraped up to 1,000 comments per trailer, keeping only those posted \emph{before} the movie's release date. Crucially, we pre-checked each trailer's upload timestamp via \texttt{videos.list}; trailers uploaded after release were skipped entirely (no pre-release comment can exist on a post-release upload). The scraper is checkpoint-based and survives daily quota exhaustion.

\paragraph{Letterboxd ratings.} We constructed candidate URLs from title and year and parsed the \texttt{aggregateRating} JSON-LD block, with \texttt{twitter:data2} as fallback. When two slug variants matched, we kept the one with more logged ratings to avoid obscure short-film collisions. Coverage: 209/210 with one manual fix (\textit{Lee} 2024 maps to \texttt{lee-2023} on Letterboxd).

\paragraph{Leakage boundary.} Our initial cutoff at the festival premiere date was too strict: \textit{Promising Young Woman} premiered at Sundance 11 months before wide release, discarding genuine pre-release comments. We switched to the wide-release date, recovering $\approx$15 movies while preserving the no-leakage guarantee for general-audience commenters.

\paragraph{Label construction.} Letterboxd scores cluster narrowly (mean 3.0, std 0.63), so equal-width bins would be severely imbalanced. We chose thresholds (Table~\ref{tab:buckets}) to produce near-equal class sizes. Filtering to movies with $\geq$30 pre-release comments and a valid rating yields the final dataset of \textbf{173 movies and $\approx$236K comments}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Structured Features}
\noindent We construct a 25-dimensional movie-level feature vector implementing all three novel components from the proposal.

\paragraph{Sentiment.} VADER \cite{hutto2014} compound scores yield mean, std, positive fraction, and negative fraction across the movie's pool.

\paragraph{Hype and negative anticipation.} We count comments containing curated hype phrases (``can't wait,'' ``masterpiece,'' ``goat'') and negative phrases (``looks bad,'' ``cash grab,'' ``flop'') and combine them: $\textit{hype\_minus\_neg} = \textit{hype\_frac} - \textit{nega\_frac}$. This is our \emph{expectation--reality proxy}.

\paragraph{Engagement.} Comment count, mean length, like-count statistics, reply rate, uppercase fraction, emoji density, and short-comment fraction capture engagement and spamminess.

\paragraph{Temporal features.} The pre-release comment timeline of each movie is divided into early/middle/late thirds, and sentiment mean and hype fraction are computed within each window---the proposal's \emph{temporal modeling} contribution.

\paragraph{Credibility-weighted sentiment.} Highly liked comments more reliably reflect mainstream opinion. We weight VADER scores $s_i$ by $w_i = \log(1 + \ell_i) + 1$ where $\ell_i$ is like count:
\[
\bar{s}_{\text{cred}} \;=\; \sum_i w_i s_i \,\Big/\, \sum_i w_i.
\]

\subsection{Prediction Models}
\noindent All models share the same movie-level 70/15/15 stratified split (seed 42).

\paragraph{Majority baseline.} Predicts the train-majority class everywhere.

\paragraph{TF-IDF + LR.} Each movie's pre-release comments are concatenated into one document. We extract word uni+bigram TF-IDF (max 20K features, $\min\!\textit{df}{=}2$, $\max\!\textit{df}{=}0.95$, English stopwords) and train multinomial L2 logistic regression with $C{=}1.0$ and \texttt{class\_weight=balanced} \cite{pedregosa2011}.

\paragraph{Structured features + LR / RF.} Standardized 25-dim features feed into balanced L2 logistic regression and a 500-tree random forest.

\paragraph{Frozen DistilBERT + LR.} Up to 200 comments per movie (preferring longer ones) are encoded through frozen \texttt{distilbert-base-uncased} \cite{sanh2019,wolf2020}. Token embeddings are mean-pooled into per-comment vectors, then averaged into a single movie vector. LR is trained on top. This is the midterm's transformer setup.

\paragraph{Fine-tuned DistilBERT, per-comment.} Per midterm reviewer feedback (``feed the whole sequence into the model, train a classifier on the [CLS] token without pooling''), we fine-tune \texttt{distilbert-base-uncased} end-to-end with a [CLS] classification head. Each comment carries its movie's bucket as a pseudo-label, converting the 121-movie problem into 80K training examples. We train 3 epochs at $\textit{lr}{=}2{\times}10^{-5}$, weight decay $0.01$, batch 32, max length 128, with early stopping on val macro F1. At inference we mean-pool per-comment softmax probabilities to the movie level and argmax.

\paragraph{Fine-tuned DistilBERT, movie-level.} As an alternative addressing the pseudo-label noise issue, we fine-tune end-to-end with one input per movie: each movie's top-60 most-liked comments concatenated and truncated at 512 tokens. Heavier regularization (weight decay $0.05$, max 8 epochs, patience 2) compensates for the small training set.

\paragraph{Ensemble.} We average class-probability outputs from TF-IDF, structured-LR, and fine-tuned movie-level DistilBERT.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\noindent We split at the movie level to prevent comment-level leakage: 121 train / 26 val / 26 test, stratified by rating bucket, seed 42. We report four metrics on the held-out test set:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Accuracy} ($\uparrow$) -- fraction of movies with the \emph{exact} correct bucket.
\item \textbf{Adjacent accuracy} ($\uparrow$) -- fraction of movies whose predicted bucket is within $\pm 1$ of the true bucket. Captures the ordinal nature of the rating scale: predicting \textit{good} when the truth is \textit{great} is treated as substantively close. A random uniform predictor on 5 classes scores 0.52.
\item \textbf{Macro F1} ($\uparrow$) -- our primary granular metric; weights all classes equally.
\item \textbf{Mean absolute label distance / MAE} ($\downarrow$) -- per midterm reviewer feedback. Computed as $\sum_{i,j} M_{ij}\cdot|i-j| / \sum_{i,j} M_{ij}$ over the confusion matrix $M$. A random uniform predictor has MAE $=1.6$; the worst case is $4.0$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Expectation--reality}
\noindent The proposal's third novel component asked whether anticipatory hype tracks with post-release reception. We compute Pearson and Spearman correlations between \texttt{hype\_minus\_neg} and the rating bucket: $r = 0.27$, $\rho = 0.24$ (Figure~\ref{fig:exp_reality}). The trend is clearly positive but noisy. Notable outliers:

\paragraph{Over-hyped flops} (high hype, low rating): \textit{Disenchanted}, \textit{The Prom}, \textit{After Ever Happy}, \textit{Halloween Ends}.

\paragraph{Sleeper hits} (low hype, high rating): \textit{Wicked Little Letters}, \textit{Dungeons \& Dragons: Honor Among Thieves}, \textit{Challengers}, \textit{The Fall Guy}.

\subsection{Error analysis}
\noindent Eight test movies were misclassified by \emph{all four} primary models (TF-IDF, Structured-LR, both DistilBERT fine-tunes): \textit{The Invisible Man}, \textit{Twisters}, \textit{The Matrix Resurrections}, \textit{The New Mutants}, \textit{The Gray Man}, \textit{A Good Person}, \textit{After Ever Happy}, and \textit{What's Love Got to Do with It?}. Aggregating the off-by-$X$ distribution across all 4 models $\times$ 26 test movies, we observe a positive mean signed error of $+0.39$, indicating a systematic tendency to \emph{over-predict} the rating bucket. This is consistent with viral trailers generating enthusiasm regardless of the movie's eventual quality. The franchise reboots in this list (\textit{Matrix Resurrections}, \textit{New Mutants}) likely fool models that learn a positive \textit{matrix}/\textit{x-men} prior from training data.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Why TF-IDF wins.} On 121 training movies with $\sim$13M characters of concatenated text per movie, lexical features are a near-perfect match for the regime: TF-IDF \emph{processes the entire text}, while a transformer is constrained by its context window (DistilBERT sees $\approx$2K of $\approx$100K characters). Movie-title and brand tokens (\textit{kraven, borderlands, snake eyes} for \textit{bad}; \textit{a24, dune, wes anderson, pixar} for \textit{great}) are heavily weighted. Removing the 5 most frequent capitalized proper nouns dropped macro F1 by $\approx$0.05, showing that title-token memorization is a real but partial driver---temporal-sentiment, hype, and engagement features still carry independent signal.

\paragraph{Why fine-tuning did not help.} The per-comment fine-tune suffers heavy pseudo-label noise: a sarcastic or off-topic comment under a \textit{great} movie still inherits the \textit{great} label. Train accuracy reached 0.975 while val macro F1 plateaued at 0.10, the textbook overfit signature. The movie-level fine-tune trades pseudo-label noise for tiny $n$ (121 examples for a 66M-parameter model). Best val F1 was reached at epoch 1 and never recovered; the 512-token context covers only $\sim$2\% of each movie's text. \emph{However}, the movie-level variant achieves the best test MAE (1.27) and adjacent accuracy (0.692) of any model: even when its bucket prediction is wrong, it is rarely far wrong---a real ordinal-quality win that strict 5-class accuracy hides but adjacent accuracy surfaces.

\paragraph{Why ensembles did not help.} The soft-vote ensemble underperformed TF-IDF alone because DistilBERT's poorly calibrated probabilities pulled the average toward the wrong class.

\paragraph{Late-window dominance.} The temporal ablation directly confirms the proposal's hypothesis: comments closer to release---when broader audiences have arrived and absorbed early reactions---are the strongest single predictor of post-release reception.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

\paragraph{Limitations.} (1) The 26-movie test set means a single misclassification shifts macro F1 by $\approx$0.015--0.02. (2) Our TMDB vote-count filter biases the dataset toward commercially prominent films, underrepresenting niche titles with extreme ratings. (3) Letterboxd scores were scraped at one point in time; recently released films have not fully converged. (4) Both fine-tuning strategies were limited to DistilBERT-base; we attempted a Longformer (4096-token context) experiment but had to abort due to swap-thrashing on a 16GB MacBook Air M4.

\paragraph{Future work.} (i) Long-context fine-tuning on a GPU machine where Longformer or BigBird can fit. (ii) Genre-aware modeling: cross-genre generalization tests by holding out one genre at a time. (iii) Calibration: align DistilBERT's class probabilities (via temperature scaling) so it can ensemble usefully. (iv) Replacing per-comment pseudo-labels with weak supervision (e.g.\ filtering to comments with high VADER magnitude) before fine-tuning.

\section{Conclusion}
\noindent We presented a six-model study of pre-release YouTube trailer comment signals for post-release Letterboxd rating prediction. On 173 movies with five rating classes, TF-IDF + LR (\textbf{0.423 acc, 0.401 macro F1, 1.19 MAE}) outperforms structured features, frozen DistilBERT, and both end-to-end DistilBERT fine-tunes. Late-window sentiment, brand vocabulary, and hype--negativity all carry meaningful signal. Our negative result on transformer fine-tuning---honest given $n{=}121$---reinforces \cite{pang2002}'s observation that lexical methods remain competitive in low-resource text classification, and points to long-context architectures or weakly supervised fine-tuning as the next steps.
